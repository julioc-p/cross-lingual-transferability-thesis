\section{Experimental Setup}\label{section::experimental_setup}

\subsection{Data Processing}
The following data processing steps were applied to ensure the quality and relevance of the text queries:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Filtering Short Queries:}  
    We removed all rows where the text queries contained fewer than four characters, as these were mostly errors originating from different datasets.
    
    \item \textbf{Classifying Statements vs. Queries:}  
    To distinguish between statements and valid queries, we employed the \textit{Gemma 27B IT model} \cite{team2024gemma} using its quantized 8-bit version to accommodate GPU memory limitations. We applied prompt engineering techniques to determine whether a given text was a statement rather than a query. If a query was classified as a statement (e.g., "The sky is blue"), it was removed from the dataset.
    
    \item \textbf{Initial Model Testing:}  
    Initially, we attempted to use \textit{BERT Large} for one-shot classification to differentiate between queries seeking information and nonsensical statements. However, this model struggled to reliably make this distinction.
    
    \item \textbf{Manual Filtering of Nonsensical Data:}  
    To further refine the dataset, we manually identified and removed commonly occurring nonsensical queries. Examples of such invalid queries include:  
    \begin{quote}
    \texttt{"not applicable", "redox is whom?", "Describe to me", "I have no idea", "does not apply", "ni idea"}
    \end{quote}
    
    \item \textbf{Translation and Expansion of the Dataset:}  
    For translation purposes, we used the \textit{Nous Research Model} \cite{teknium2024hermes} in 4-bit precision. This model was selected based on translation benchmarks, where it ranked among the best freely available models. Due to API limitations, we did not use commercial translation services such as Google Translate or DeepL, as they were not feasible for translating hundreds of thousands of data points.
    
    \item \textbf{Addition of German Data and Quality Filtering:}  
    The translation process added approximately 200,000 additional rows in the German language. However, we observed that longer translated queries often suffered from poor quality, requiring further filtering. For instance, some translations exhibited excessive repetition, as seen in the following example:  
    \begin{quote}
    "Hatte der Goldrausch Charlie Chaplin bearbeitet, geschrieben und für Charlie Chaplin, Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin und Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie Chaplin Charlie ..."
    \end{quote}
    Due to such issues, we applied length-based filtering to remove problematic translations.
\end{enumerate}

These preprocessing steps significantly improved the overall quality of the dataset by removing incomplete, irrelevant, and low-quality queries while expanding it with translated data.
\subsection{Training}
\subsubsection{Training Overview and Model Versions}

The fine-tuning experiments in this thesis centered on two primary base models: Mistral 7B v0.1 \cite{jiang2023mistral7b} and Occiglot-7b-eu5 \cite{avramidis-etal-2024-occiglot}.
The training process and the resultant fine-tuned models can be broadly categorized into two main iterations, designated as v1 and v2.
However, it is important to note that the development was an incremental process, with insights from earlier experiments informing subsequent refinements.

The v1 iteration itself comprised two sub-versions (v1.0 and v1.1), leading to a total of 10 distinct, functional fine-tuned models when considering all languages and variations throughout variations v1 and v2.
For clarity and conciseness, when "v1" is referenced throughout this thesis without further specification, it pertains to the final v1.1 models, which demonstrated slightly improved performance compared to v1.0 as will be described in this chapter.
The earlier v1.0 models and their configurations can be accessed through the commit history of the respective model repositories on the Hugging Face Hub (See Appendix \ref{appendix:v1_huggingface}).

The v1 set of experiments served primarily an exploratory purpose: to establish a baseline understanding of the general efficacy of fine-tuning these models for the text-to-SPARQL task with the dataset, and to identify initial challenges.
Consequently, the optimization was less of a focus in v1, particularly in the v1.0 stage which were only fine-tuned in English.

The v2 iteration was developed to address the limitations observed in v1 and to achieve higher performance.
A significant distinction was the incorporation of Wikidata entity and relationship ID mappings directly into the training input for the v2 models as context.
This approach was adopted not to supply them with the necessary explicit grounding for entities and relationships mentioned in the natural language queries.
The rationale was that if a model has not encountered specific token-to-ID mappings during its pre-training, it cannot be expected to generate them correctly.
Ideally, such mappings would have been part of the pre-training data; however, given the resource constraints of this thesis, providing them during fine-tuning and evaluation was an attempt to emulate the knowledge state of a model that had been pre-trained with this information.
This also allowed for an investigation into the hypothesis that the base Mistral model might have already been exposed to such mappings.
As will be discuss later, the performance increase (approximately fourfold in F1 score) suggest this exposure was likely minimal or incidental, something that cannot be asserted since by public statements from the Mistral AI team, the specifics of their training data are not disclosed due to the competitive nature of the field \cite{Arthurmensch_HF_MistralData_2023}.

Another key difference in the v2 experiments was the use of the base, non-instruction-tuned versions of the LLMs.
This decision was made to allow for greater control over the input format, specifically to enable the inclusion of a custom system prompt for Mistral, as its instruction-tuned variant does not natively support it, and it was hypothesized that a well-crafted system prompt could guide the model more effectively during inference.
In contrast, the v1 experiments utilized the instruction-tuned versions of both models.

The v2 experiments also incorporated more best practices and advanced training techniques, such as an evaluation loop with the BLEU metric, early stopping criteria, NEFTune noise injection, and more targeted hyperparameter adjustments based on relevant literature, all of which will be detailed further in this chapter.

To ensure clarity and facilitate replication, the specific Hugging Face model identifiers used are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{v1 Experiments (Instruction-Tuned Models):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Mistral: \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1}
        \item Occiglot: \texttt{occiglot/occiglot-7b-eu5-instruct}
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{v2 Experiments (Base/Raw Models):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Mistral: \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.1}
        \item Occiglot: \texttt{occiglot/occiglot-7b-eu5}
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Computational Resources and Environment}

All model fine-tuning experiments were conducted on a High-Performance Computing (HPC) cluster provided by the German Research Center for Artificial Intelligence (DFKI).
The cluster offered access to a diverse range of NVIDIA GPUs, including A100, H100, and V100 models.
Due to high demand and consequently longer queue times for the A100 and H100 resources, NVIDIA V100 GPUs with 32 GB of GPU RAM were consistently utilized for all training runs to ensure timely and predictable access.
Each training job was allocated approximately 60 GB of system RAM, which proved sufficient for the chosen model sizes and batch configurations.

For simplicity and given that the model sizes were manageable on a single GPU, distributed training across multiple nodes or GPUs was not employed; all experiments were run on a single-node, single-GPU basis.
Job submission and management on the HPC cluster were handled using the Slurm Workload Manager.
The training environment was containerized using NVIDIA's Enroot container platform. The base Docker image for all training environments was \texttt{nvcr.io/nvidia/cuda:11.8.0-devel-ubuntu20.04}.
Within this containerized environment, all necessary Python libraries and dependencies required by the training scripts were installed using their latest stable versions available at the time of the experiments.


\subsubsection{Fine-tuning}

To efficiently fine-tune the selected models within the available computational constraints, all experiments leveraged the Hugging Face ecosystem, particularly the Parameter-Efficient Fine-tuning (PEFT) library \cite{peft} and its integration of Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA) \cite{dettmers2023qlora}.
This integration implies the interplay of the BitsAndBytes, transformers, trl and peft libraries \cite{hf_blog_4bit_qlora_2023}.

The decision to employ 4-bit quantization was primarily driven by the 32 GB memory capacity of the V100 GPUs.
While both models require approximately 14-15 GB of GPU RAM in their native bfloat16 precision, loading the model, its optimizer states, and the training data batches for fine-tuning necessitates further memory optimization.
Quantization to 4-bit precision reduces the model's memory requirement substantially, allowing for the accommodation of larger batch sizes and longer sequence lengths, and ensuring that the training data vectors and gradients could fit within the GPU memory.

\textbf{Initial parameters (v1.0)}

The first fine-tuning experiments commenced with a set of baseline hyperparameters for the LoRA configuration and the general training arguments.

The initial LoRA setup was guided by a combination of values showcased in the hyperparameter search of the QLoRA paper \cite{dettmers2023qlora}.
The first choice of layers  (specifically, \texttt{q\_proj, k\_proj, v\_proj, o\_proj, gate\_proj, up\_proj, down\_proj, lm\_head}) was mainly driven by the huggingface community suggestions on finetuning Mistral 7B models like \cite{roggendorff2024finetuning}.
The configuration applied to the target modules was as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{r} (LoRA rank): 64
    \item \texttt{lora\_alpha} (LoRA alpha, scaling factor): 16
    \item \texttt{lora\_dropout} (Dropout probability for LoRA layers): 0.1
    \item \texttt{bias}: "none"
    \item \texttt{task\_type}: "CAUSAL\_LM"
\end{itemize}

For the main training arguments, the initial settings were largely adapted from the parameters reported for the continual pre-training of the Occiglot models \cite{occiglot2024occiglot7b}, under the rationale that these had proven effective for adapting the base Mistral architecture.
However, significant adjustments were necessary to accommodate the single-GPU fine-tuning setup and the specific constraints of the available V100 hardware, particularly concerning batch size.
The key training arguments for the v1 experiments were:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{num\_train\_epochs}: 5
    \item \texttt{per\_device\_train\_batch\_size}: 1
    \item \texttt{per\_device\_eval\_batch\_size}: 1
    \item \texttt{gradient\_accumulation\_steps}: 8 (to achieve an effective batch size of 8)
    \item \texttt{gradient\_checkpointing}: True (to save memory)
    \item \texttt{optim}: "paged\_adamw\_32bit"
    \item \texttt{learning\_rate}: 1e-5
    \item \texttt{weight\_decay}: 0.05
    \item \texttt{bf16}: False (NVIDIA V100 does not support bfloat16 \cite{meng2024bfloat16})
    \item \texttt{max\_grad\_norm}: 1.0
    \item \texttt{max\_steps}: -1 (i.e., driven by \texttt{num\_train\_epochs})
    \item \texttt{warmup\_ratio}: 0.01
    \item \texttt{lr\_scheduler\_type}: "cosine"
    \item \texttt{group\_by\_length}: True (to optimize padding and speed up training)
    \item \texttt{max\_seq\_length}: None (allowing the model to handle variable sequence lengths up to its inherent limit, typically 2048 or 4096 tokens for these models)
    \item \texttt{packing}: False
    \item \texttt{device\_map}: \{"": 0\} (to ensure the model is loaded onto the primary GPU)
\end{itemize}

For the BitsAndBytes configuration for 4-bit quantization was set to dequantize for computations to float16.

A crucial adaptation from the Occiglot pre-training setup was the batch size.
Due to GPU memory limitations when fine-tuning the models, a \texttt{per\_device\_train\_batch\_size} of 1 was used. 
To compensate for this small physical batch size and simulate a larger effective batch size for more stable gradient updates, \texttt{gradient\_accumulation\_steps} was set to 8.

For these initial v1.0 fine-tuning runs, the full dataset (comprising nearly 900,000 examples) was not used.
Instead, for only English, a subset of 35,000 randomly sampled examples was selected for training.
This was done to expedite the exploratory phase and allow for quicker iteration cycles.
It is important to note that this training subset still contained a diverse range of queries originating from approximately 15 different knowledge graphs.
While SPARQL is a standardized query language, the specific entities, relationships, naming conventions, and structural query patterns can vary considerably across these different knowledge graphs.
This initial training took about 19 hours.
